# "Tracker" or "log"? Search titles, App Store name and keywords

Checked 3 October 2026 by the research agent. Question from the founder: should the website's Google title and meta description, and the App Store name, say "mileage tracker" or "mileage log"? We don't want to sound like Big Brother.

## The answer in short

- **Keep "tracking" and "tracker" out of everything people read. Put "tracker" only in the App Store keywords field, which nobody sees.** That gets us found for "mileage tracker" on the App Store without saying it.
- **"Mileage tracker" is the category's name.** Every big rival uses it in its App Store name (MileIQ, Driversnote, TripLog, Everlance, Stride, MileClear), and reviewers say "track" about three times as often as "log". So we can't ignore the term. We just don't need to say it out loud.
- **Australia and Canada say "logbook".** In Australia, Driversnote and Everlance rename their apps to "Vehicle Logbook" and "Mileage Logbook". In Canada their subtitles say "CRA vehicle logbook". "Log" is the natural word there, not a compromise.
- **On iPhone, "track" is a loaded word.** Apple uses "track" for following people across apps for ads. Its permission prompt asks whether an app may "track your activity", and every App Store page has a "Data Used to Track You" label. Our privacy story is the opposite of that, so "tracker" in our name works against our own pitch.
- **On Google, we lose very little.** Google doesn't use the meta description for ranking. A new site won't beat MileIQ, Driversnote and Everlance for "mileage tracker" any time soon, whatever the title says. Phrases like "free mileage log app" and "HMRC mileage log" are easier to win.
- **One gap:** I couldn't get search-volume numbers. Google Trends, keyword tools and Apple's search suggestions are all blocked from this machine, so the demand section below uses indirect evidence. The guess that "mileage tracker" gets more searches than "mileage log" is medium confidence, not measured.

## 1. Search demand

**What I could not measure:** Google Trends (trends.google.com), keyword tools (Semrush, Ahrefs, keywordtool.io, Wordtracker), Google, Bing and DuckDuckGo autocomplete, and Apple's App Store search-hints API all returned "blocked by egress proxy" on 3 Oct 2026. So I have **no volume or Trends numbers** for any country, and I have not made any up.

What this suggests (inference, **medium-low confidence**):

- The same few big apps rank for all five phrases. Apple treats them as one family of searches, so a name with "Mileage" in it plus "tracker" in the keywords field should cover all five.
- "Mileage logger" and "mileage log" are where small apps with those exact words still reach the top 3. That fits lower competition, and probably lower volume, but this data can't separate the two.
- Leave out "mile tracker": Apple fills it with fitness apps.
- Revealed preference: the market leaders, who can see Apple's search popularity scores, all chose "Mileage Tracker" for their names in the US, UK and Canada. That points to "tracker" being the bigger search term there. In Australia, the two leaders who localise chose "Logbook" instead, which points to "logbook" being the term Australians search for. The ATO itself calls it the "logbook method".
- Users' own words: in the 84 unique App Store reviews shown on the US and UK pages of MileIQ, Driversnote, Everlance, Stride and TripLog, "track…" appears 118 times, "log…" 40 times and "record…" 35 times.

**To close the gap** (for marketing, not urgent): compare the five phrases in Google Trends for GB, US, CA and AU over the last 5 years, and check Apple's search popularity scores if the founder already has an Apple Ads account. Don't open one just for this.

## 3. Does "tracking" put people off?

- **Apple makes "track" mean surveillance on iPhone. High confidence, primary source.** Apple's definition: "Tracking refers to the act of linking user or device data collected from your app with user or device data collected from other companies' apps, websites, or offline properties for targeted advertising or advertising measurement purposes." The App Tracking Transparency prompt lets users choose whether an app may "track their activity across other companies' apps and websites". Every product page has a "Data Used to Track You" privacy label; it's on MileIQ's US page, for example. Sources: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/user-privacy-and-data-use/ and https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id578830929. Our own home page comparison table already uses this label as a row ("Data used to track you across other companies' apps"). A name with "Tracker" in it sits awkwardly next to that row.
- **Research says "tracking" makes people react badly. Low-medium confidence: search summaries only, papers not opened** (sciencedirect.com, par.nsf.gov and bath.ac.uk are blocked here):
  - a study of iOS privacy labels: "I would not install an app with this label" (https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10577930) found that labels saying data is used for cross-app tracking sharply cut willingness to install. The summary's "304 times" figure is unverified, so don't quote it;
  - a University of Bath study found many phone users unclear about what "app tracking" means (https://www.bath.ac.uk/announcements/research-shows-mobile-phone-users-do-not-understand-what-data-they-might-be-sharing/);
  - a 2026 study of ATT found its negative-toned prompts raised privacy concern (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S030859612600042X).
- **Carers and employees: being tracked by an employer is a live worry.** A QuickBooks survey, quoted in a UK employment blog, found 70% of employees not yet asked to use GPS felt it would invade their privacy, and 68% worried about being tracked after work (https://www.timekeeper.co.uk/blog/posts/gps-employee-tracking-apps). **Low confidence:** this is a vendor blog quoting a vendor survey. UK home-care workers are already GPS-monitored by agencies through "electronic call monitoring" (for example https://www.birdie.care/blog/domiciliary-care-call-monitoring-system), so for them "tracker" can sound like the boss's tool.
- **Counter-evidence: in practice, "mileage tracker" reads as a neutral category name.** None of the 84 visible reviews of the big five raised privacy, spying or "creepy". The leaders' ratings are high (4.7 to 4.8) despite "Tracker" in their names. Reviewers themselves say "track". **Medium confidence:** the App Store shows only "most helpful" reviews, and people put off by a name don't install, so they don't review.

Overall: "mileage tracker" isn't a turn-off for the general market. But for our pitch (nothing leaves your phone, nobody sees your drives), "track" is the one word Apple has taught iPhone users to say no to. Avoiding it in what people read costs little and fits the brand.

## 4. Can we rank for "mileage tracker" without saying it?

**App Store: yes, mostly. High confidence on the rules, medium on how words combine.**

- Apple: search is based on "text relevance (matches for your app's title, subtitle, keywords, and primary category)" plus downloads and ratings. The keywords field has 100 characters, separated by commas, and "Don't repeat any words included in your app name, subtitle, or category." It also says not to repeat plurals. Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/search/
- Guideline 2.3.7: "assign keywords that accurately describe your app", with no competitor names or "irrelevant phrases"; subtitles must not "reference other apps". Source: https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ ("tracker" is an accurate generic description, so it's allowed. "MileIQ alternative" in the subtitle is not.)
- With "Mileage" in the name and "tracker" in the keywords field, a search for "mileage tracker" matches both words. Apple doesn't document how words combine across fields. This is standard App Store optimisation practice, and the search results above are consistent with it (e.g. "Log-Book" ranks for "mileage log app"). **Medium confidence.**
- **Website: partly.**
  - The meta description doesn't affect Google ranking, only the snippet and click-through. Source: Google Search Central, "Meta tags and attributes that Google supports", https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/special-tags (seen via search summary, since developers.google.com is blocked here). **High confidence;** Google has said this publicly for years.
  - The `<meta name="keywords">` tag is ignored by Google: https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2009/09/google-does-not-use-keywords-meta-tag. Hidden text is against Google's spam policies. So there is no invisible field on the web like Apple's keywords field.
  - The title is visible (browser tab and Google result) and is the strongest on-page signal. Ranking for the exact phrase "mileage tracker" on Google would need the word in the title or body copy. **Medium confidence**, standard SEO practice.
  - "A mileage tracker alternative" would put the word in visible copy, so it breaks the wording rule. It also tells readers we're a "tracker" too. It's not worth it in a title. On a comparison page, "an alternative to MileIQ" (no "tracker") is fine.
- **Realistic view:** the top results for "mileage tracker" on Google are big brands with years of links. A new static site won't reach page one on the title alone. The phrases we can win are long-tail ones such as "free mileage log app", "HMRC mileage log app", "mileage log for couriers", "ATO car logbook app" and "CRA vehicle logbook app". Those match how we already talk. **Medium confidence; this is a judgement, not measured.**

## 5. Recommendation

**Never "tracker" or "tracking" in anything people read. "Tracker" goes only in the App Store keywords field.** Lead with "mileage log" (UK and US) and "logbook" (Canada and Australia).

### App Store (`milemint/store.config.json`)

- **Name, all four stores:** `MileSprout: Mileage Log` (23 chars). This replaces "MileMint: Mileage Tracker", which also still has the old brand name.
- **Subtitles:** none of the current ones say "track", so they can stay. One tweak: the UK subtitle "Automatic HMRC Mileage Log" repeats "Mileage Log" from the new name, which wastes search space. Use `Automatic HMRC Business Miles` (29) instead. Canada's "Automatic CRA Vehicle Logbook" and Australia's "ATO Car Log, Cents per Km" fit how those markets search, so keep them.
- **Keywords (add `tracker`; drop words that repeat the name):**
  - US: `irs,deduction,1099,gig,driver,rideshare,delivery,self employed,writeoff,logbook,schedule c,tracker` (98)
  - UK: `hmrc,self assessment,55p,sole trader,allowance,expenses,claim,logbook,delivery,courier,tracker` (94). "Mileage allowance" becomes "allowance", because "mileage" is in the name. "Business miles" moves into the subtitle.
  - Canada: current list plus `,tracker` (93)
  - Australia: current list plus `,tracker` (95)

### For the founder to decide (brand)

The wording rule bans "tracking" in what people read. Only the founder can make an exception, and **I'm not recommending one.** The one version I'd consider is a single FAQ line that turns the word around: "Is MileSprout a mileage tracker? It does the same job: it logs your drives by itself. But nothing tracks you: your trips stay on your iPhone." That would give Google the phrase in the page text. The cost is saying "track" twice. Skip it unless "mileage tracker" proves to be a big Google source later.
